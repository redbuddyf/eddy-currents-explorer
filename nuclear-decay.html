1.1 Inside the Nucleus

Every atom has a tiny core at its centre — the nucleus. It contains two types of particles: positively charged protons and electrically neutral neutrons. Together, these are called nucleons. The number of protons determines what element the atom is; the number of neutrons determines which isotope of that element it is.

We describe any nucleus using standard nuclear notation. The atomic number (Z) is the number of protons. The mass number (A) is the total number of protons plus neutrons. The number of neutrons is therefore N = A − Z.

AZX
X = element symbol  |  Z = atomic number (protons)  |  A = mass number (nucleons)

Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have different numbers of neutrons. For example, carbon-12 (126C) has 6 protons and 6 neutrons, while carbon-14 (146C) has 6 protons and 8 neutrons. Carbon-12 is stable; carbon-14 is radioactive and decays over time. An unstable isotope is called a radioisotope.

Key distinctions

  • Nucleon = any particle in the nucleus (proton or neutron)
  • Isotope = same element, different neutron count
  • Radioisotope = an unstable isotope that undergoes radioactive decay
  • Nuclide = a specific combination of protons and neutrons

1.2 Nuclear Forces & Binding Energy

The nucleus of an atom is a battlefield of competing forces. On one side, the strong nuclear force — the most powerful force in nature — pulls protons and neutrons together, binding them into a dense cluster just femtometres across. On the other side, the electromagnetic force relentlessly pushes protons apart, since each carries a positive charge and like charges repel.

For light nuclei, the strong force dominates. Add too many protons, however, and the cumulative electrostatic repulsion grows faster than the strong force can compensate. Beyond a certain point — roughly iron-56 on the periodic table — adding more nucleons actually reduces the binding energy per particle. The nucleus becomes unstable. It must shed mass and energy to reach a more stable configuration.

This is the origin of radioactive decay: an unstable nucleus spontaneously transforms into a different nucleus, releasing energy and/or particles in the process. The decay is random for any individual nucleus, but follows precise statistical laws when applied to large populations.

The Four Fundamental Forces in the Nucleus

Only two of the four fundamental forces operate inside the nucleus. Gravity is impossibly weak at nuclear scales — a proton-proton gravitational attraction is roughly 1038 times weaker than their electromagnetic repulsion. The weak nuclear force, while important in beta decay, has an even shorter range than the strong force and does not bind nucleons together.

The strong nuclear force is unique: it is charge-independent (it acts equally between proton-proton, neutron-neutron, and proton-neutron pairs) and saturates (each nucleon only interacts with its immediate neighbours, not the entire nucleus). This saturation explains why nuclear density is roughly constant — all nuclei have approximately the same density as a droplet of water, compressed into a sphere 10,000 times smaller in radius.

The binding energy puzzle

  • If you add up the masses of all the individual protons and neutrons in a nucleus, you get a larger number than the actual mass of the nucleus.
  • The difference is called the mass defect (Δm).
  • Einstein's equation E = mc² tells us this missing mass was converted into the energy that binds the nucleus together — the binding energy.
  • The more binding energy per nucleon, the more stable the nucleus.
E = mc² → BE = [Z mp + N mn − mnucleus] c²

Heavy water

A compound of oxygen and deuterium has identical chemical properties to ordinary water. However, the molecular mass of ordinary water is about 18 (16 + 1 + 1), while the molecular mass of water containing deuterium is 20 (16 + 2 + 2). Thus, water that contains deuterium has a higher density (by about 11%) and is commonly known as 'heavy water'. One use of heavy water is in nuclear reactors.

1.3 Why Nuclei Decay

The nucleus of an atom is a battlefield of competing forces. On one side, the strong nuclear force — the most powerful force in nature — pulls protons and neutrons together, binding them into a dense cluster just femtometres across. On the other side, the electromagnetic force relentlessly pushes protons apart, since each carries a positive charge and like charges repel.

For light nuclei, the strong force dominates. Add too many protons, however, and the cumulative electrostatic repulsion grows faster than the strong force can compensate. Beyond a certain point — roughly iron-56 on the periodic table — adding more nucleons actually reduces the binding energy per particle. The nucleus becomes unstable. It must shed mass and energy to reach a more stable configuration.

This is the origin of radioactive decay: an unstable nucleus spontaneously transforms into a different nucleus, releasing energy and/or particles in the process. The decay is random for any individual nucleus, but follows precise statistical laws when applied to large populations.

What makes a nucleus unstable?

  • Proton-neutron ratio: Light stable nuclei have roughly equal protons and neutrons. Heavier nuclei need extra neutrons to dilute proton repulsion.
  • Total nucleon number: Nuclei heavier than lead-208 are inherently unstable due to the short range of the strong force.
  • Shell structure: Nucleons occupy discrete energy levels. "Magic numbers" (2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126) confer extra stability.
  • Pairing effect: Even numbers of protons and neutrons are more stable than odd numbers.

The Valley of Stability

Not all combinations of protons and neutrons are equally stable. If you plot every known nuclide on a graph with neutrons (N) on one axis and protons (Z) on the other, the stable nuclides form a narrow band called the valley of stability.

For light nuclei, the stable ones have roughly equal numbers of protons and neutrons (N ≈ Z). As nuclei get heavier, the stable ones need proportionally more neutrons to dilute the proton repulsion. The valley curves upward — away from the N = Z line — for heavy elements.

Nuclides above the valley (too many neutrons) tend to undergo beta-minus decay to convert a neutron into a proton. Nuclides below the valley (too many protons) tend to undergo beta-plus decay or alpha decay to reduce their proton count.

1.4 Modes of Radioactive Decay

Alpha decay is the emission of a helium-4 nucleus from a heavy parent nucleus. The alpha particle consists of 2 protons and 2 neutrons — an extraordinarily stable configuration thanks to its doubly magic structure and high binding energy per nucleon (7.07 MeV).

Alpha decay occurs predominantly in very heavy nuclei (Z > 82) where the electrostatic repulsion between protons becomes overwhelming. By ejecting an alpha particle, the parent nucleus reduces both its mass number (A decreases by 4) and atomic number (Z decreases by 2), shifting it toward the valley of stability.

23892U → 23490Th + 42He + 4.27 MeV
Uranium-238 decays to Thorium-234, releasing an alpha particle and 4.27 MeV of kinetic energy.

Quantum Tunnelling

Here is where alpha decay becomes truly fascinating. The alpha particle does not have enough energy to classically overcome the nuclear potential barrier. Yet it escapes anyway. How?

The answer is quantum tunnelling. In quantum mechanics, particles exist as probability waves. There is a non-zero probability that the alpha particle can "tunnel" through the barrier — a region where classical physics says it cannot exist. The thicker and higher the barrier, the lower the tunnelling probability, and thus the longer the half-life.

This explains the extraordinary range of alpha decay half-lives: from microseconds for polonium-212 to 4.5 billion years for uranium-238. The same physical process, differing only in the tunnelling probability.

Alpha Particle Properties

  • Charge: +2e (helium nucleus without electrons)
  • Mass: 4.0015 u (roughly 7300× the electron mass)
  • Typical kinetic energy: 4–9 MeV
  • Range in air: 2–10 cm
  • Range in tissue: 0.03–0.1 mm (cannot penetrate skin)
  • Highly ionising but low penetrating power

Physics in Action

Radioactive lamps

The wicks or mantles used in old-style camping lamps are slightly radioactive. They contain a radioisotope of thorium, an alpha-particle emitter. They have not been banned from sale so far because they contain only small amounts of the radioisotope and can be used safely by taking simple precautions such as washing hands and avoiding inhalation or ingestion.

However, a scientist from the Australian National University in Canberra has called for these mantles to be banned because they tend to crumble and turn to dust as they age. If this dust were inhaled, alpha particles could settle in someone's lung tissue, possibly causing cancers to form.

Physics in Action

Smoke detectors

Domestic smoke detectors contain a tiny amount of americium-241, an alpha emitter. The alpha particles ionise air molecules between two electrodes, creating a small electric current. When smoke enters the chamber, it absorbs the alpha particles, reducing the current and triggering the alarm. Because alpha particles cannot penetrate the plastic casing, the device is completely safe in normal use.

Beta-Minus Decay

Beta-minus decay occurs in neutron-rich nuclei — isotopes with too many neutrons relative to protons. Inside the nucleus, a neutron transforms into a proton, emitting an electron (called a beta particle in this context) and an electron antineutrino.

This decay is mediated by the weak nuclear force, the only force that can change one flavour of quark into another. Inside the neutron, a down quark (charge −1/3) converts to an up quark (charge +2/3), changing the baryon from a neutron to a proton. The charge difference of +1 is carried away by the emitted electron. The antineutrino ensures conservation of lepton number.

146C → 147N + e− + νe + 0.156 MeV
Carbon-14 decays to Nitrogen-14. The atomic number increases by 1 while mass number stays the same.

The Neutrino Problem

The continuous energy spectrum of beta particles was one of the great puzzles of early 20th-century physics. Alpha and gamma emission produce discrete energy peaks, but beta particles emerge with a continuous spread of energies from near-zero up to a maximum value. Where did the "missing" energy go?

Wolfgang Pauli proposed in 1930 that an undetected, electrically neutral particle carried away the remaining energy. Enrico Fermi named it the neutrino — "little neutral one" — and developed the theory of beta decay. Neutrinos interact so weakly with matter that a typical neutrino could pass through a light-year of lead without stopping. They were not directly detected until 1956.

Why the antineutrino matters

  • Conserves lepton number: electron (lepton number +1) + antineutrino (lepton number −1) = 0, matching the neutron's lepton number of 0.
  • Explains the continuous energy spectrum: the kinetic energy is shared randomly between the electron and antineutrino.
  • Carries away spin angular momentum: the neutron, proton, and electron spins cannot balance without it.

Physics in Action

PET scans

Positron emission tomography (PET) uses fluorine-18, a beta-plus emitter, to image metabolic activity in the body. The emitted positron quickly meets an electron and they annihilate, producing two gamma rays travelling in opposite directions. Detectors around the patient pick up these coincident gamma rays and build a 3D image of where the tracer has accumulated — revealing tumours, brain activity, or heart function.

Beta-Plus Decay

Beta-plus decay is the mirror image of beta-minus decay. It occurs in proton-rich nuclei — isotopes with too many protons relative to neutrons. A proton converts into a neutron, emitting a positron (the antimatter counterpart of the electron) and an electron neutrino.

Beta-plus decay can only occur when the parent atom's mass exceeds the daughter atom's mass by at least twice the electron rest mass (1.022 MeV), because a proton is slightly lighter than a neutron. This energy threshold means beta-plus decay is less common than beta-minus decay.

189F → 188O + e+ + νe + 0.635 MeV
Fluorine-18 decays to Oxygen-18, releasing a positron and neutrino.

Electron Capture: An Alternative Path

Proton-rich nuclei have another decay route: electron capture. Instead of emitting a positron, the nucleus captures one of its own inner-shell electrons. The captured electron combines with a proton to form a neutron and a neutrino. The atomic number decreases by 1, just as in beta-plus decay, but no positron is emitted.

Electron capture becomes increasingly favoured over positron emission for heavier nuclei, because inner-shell electrons spend more time inside the nucleus (due to relativistic effects and orbital penetration). Some nuclei, like potassium-40, can decay by both beta-minus decay and electron capture — a remarkable example of nuclear versatility.

Gamma Decay

Gamma decay is fundamentally different from alpha and beta decay. It does not change the identity of the nucleus — no protons or neutrons are lost or converted. Instead, an excited nucleus releases excess energy as a high-energy photon, dropping to a lower, more stable energy state.

Gamma rays are electromagnetic radiation, just like visible light or X-rays, but with far higher energy (typically 10 keV to 10 MeV) and correspondingly shorter wavelength. They are the most penetrating form of radioactive emission, requiring dense shielding such as lead or thick concrete to attenuate.

99m43Tc → 9943Tc + γ (140 keV)
Technetium-99m decays to its ground state by emitting a 140 keV gamma photon. The "m" denotes a metastable (excited) state.

Why Gamma Rays Are So Penetrating

Alpha particles are massive and doubly charged — they interact strongly with matter via the electromagnetic force, losing energy rapidly through ionisation. Beta particles, being much lighter, also interact electromagnetically but penetrate further because their smaller mass makes them harder to "stop."

Gamma photons, however, carry no charge and have zero rest mass. They interact with matter primarily through three processes:

  1. Photoelectric effect: The gamma photon transfers all its energy to an atomic electron, ejecting it.
  2. Compton scattering: The photon bounces off an electron, losing some energy and changing direction.
  3. Pair production: At energies above 1.022 MeV, the photon can spontaneously create an electron-positron pair near a nucleus.

Because these interactions are probabilistic rather than continuous, gamma rays follow an exponential attenuation law: I = I0e−μx. Half-value layers for typical medical gamma rays are measured in centimetres of lead.

Gamma knife surgery

Despite its name, the gamma knife does not cut. It uses 201 precisely focused beams of cobalt-60 gamma radiation to treat brain tumours. 1.5 Half-Life & Decay Mathematics

Radioactive decay is a random process. We cannot predict when any individual nucleus will decay. But for a large population of identical nuclei, the decay follows precise statistical laws — specifically, an exponential decay curve.

The half-life (t1/2) is the time required for half of the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay. After one half-life, 50% remain. After two half-lives, 25%. After three, 12.5%. This pattern continues indefinitely — in theory, a tiny fraction of the original sample always remains, though in practice it eventually becomes undetectable.

N(t) = N0 · (1/2)t / t1/2 = N0 · e−λt
N(t) = number remaining at time t. N0 = initial number. λ = decay constant = ln(2) / t1/2.

The decay constant λ represents the probability per unit time that any given nucleus will decay. It is related to half-life by λ = ln(2) / t1/2 ≈ 0.693 / t1/2. A large λ means rapid decay (short half-life). A small λ means slow decay (long half-life).

Activity & the Becquerel

The activity of a radioactive sample is the number of decays per second. Its SI unit is the becquerel (Bq), where 1 Bq = 1 decay per second. Activity is proportional to the number of remaining nuclei: A = λN.

Activity also decays exponentially: A(t) = A0e−λt. This is why radioactive samples become less dangerous over time — not because individual decays become less energetic, but because there are simply fewer decays happening each second.

Key stable isotopes to remember

  • Helium-4 (Z=2, N=2) — doubly magic, alpha particle itself
  • Oxygen-16 (Z=8, N=8) — doubly magic, extremely stable
  • Calcium-40 (Z=20, N=20) — doubly magic
  • Iron-56 — peak binding energy per nucleon
  • Lead-208 (Z=82, N=126) — heaviest stable nucleus, doubly magic

Physics File

Measuring radioactivity

A Geiger counter can be used to record the number of radioactive decays occurring in a sample each second. It measures the activity of the sample. Over time, the activity of a sample of a radioisotope will decrease. More and more of the radioactive nuclei will have decayed, and at some point it will no longer emit radiation.

If a sample of polonium-218 (t1/2 = 3 minutes) has an initial activity of 2000 Bq, then after one half-life its activity will be 1000 Bq. After a further 3 minutes, the activity of the sample will have reduced to 500 Bq, and after a further 3 minutes it will be 250 Bq, and so on.

Activity and the Becquerel

The activity of a radioactive sample is the number of decays per second. It is measured in becquerels (Bq), where 1 Bq = 1 decay per second. Activity is related to the number of nuclei and the decay constant by:

A = λN
Activity = decay constant × number of radioactive nuclei

Decay Chains

When a radionuclide decays, its daughter nucleus is usually itself radioactive. This daughter will then decay to a granddaughter nucleus, which may also be radioactive, and so on. This is called a decay series. Over time, the original parent isotope is gradually converted through a chain of decays until a stable isotope is reached.

For example, uranium-238 undergoes a series of 14 decays (8 alpha and 6 beta-minus) before finally reaching stable lead-206. The entire series takes billions of years.

1.6 Applications of Nuclear Physics

Physics in Action

How technetium is produced

Technetium-99m is the most widely used radioisotope in nuclear medicine. It is used for diagnosing and treating cancer. However, this radioisotope decays relatively quickly and so usually needs to be produced close to where it will be used. Technetium-99m is produced in small nuclear generators that are located in hospitals around the country. In this process, the radioisotope molybdenum-99 is used as the parent nuclide. Molybdenum-99 decays by beta emission to form a relatively stable (or metastable) isotope of technetium, technetium-99m.

9942Mo → 99m43Tc + 0−1β + ν

Technetium-99m is flushed from the generator using a saline solution. The radioisotope is then diluted and attached to an appropriate chemical compound before being administered to the patient as a tracer. Technetium-99m is purely a gamma emitter. This makes it very useful as a diagnostic tool for locating and treating cancer. Its decay equation is:

99m43Tc → 9943Tc + γ

Physics in Action

Detecting radiation

Our bodies cannot detect alpha, beta or gamma radiation. Therefore, a number of devices have been developed to detect and measure radiation.

A common detector is the Geiger counter. This can be used:

  • by geologists searching for radioactive minerals such as uranium
  • to monitor radiation levels in mines
  • to measure the level of radiation after a nuclear accident, such as the accident at Fukushima, Japan, in 2011
  • to check the safety of nuclear reactors
  • to monitor radiation levels in hospitals and factories

People who work in occupations that involve ongoing exposure to levels of ionising radiation usually pin a small radiation-monitoring device to their clothing. This is usually a thermoluminescent dosimeter (TLD). These are used by personnel in nuclear power plants, radiotherapy departments at hospitals, airport security gates and uranium mines.

Physics in Action

Radiocarbon dating

Carbon dating is a technique used by archaeologists to determine the ages of fossils and ancient objects that were made from plant matter. This method involves measuring and comparing the proportion of two isotopes of carbon, carbon-12 and carbon-14, in the specimen.

Carbon-12 is a stable isotope but carbon-14 is radioactive. Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5730 years and decays by beta-minus emission to nitrogen-14. Its decay equation is:

146C → 147N + 0−1β− + ν

Both carbon-12 and carbon-14 can combine with other atoms in the environment. For example, they both combine with oxygen to form carbon dioxide. Plants and animals take in carbon-based molecules from the air and food. This means that all living organisms contain the same percentage of carbon-14. In the environment, the production of carbon-14 is matched by its decay and so the proportion of carbon-14 atoms to carbon-12 remains constant.

After an organism dies, the amount of carbon-14 it contains will decrease as these atoms decay to form nitrogen-14 and are not replaced from the environment. The number of atoms of carbon-12 does not change as carbon-12 is a stable atom. So, over time, the proportion of carbon-14 to carbon-12 atoms decreases. The proportion of carbon-14 to carbon-12 in a dead organism can be compared with that found in living organisms and the approximate age of the specimen can be determined from the half-life of carbon-14.

1.7 Chapter Review

Summary

  • The nucleus contains protons and neutrons, collectively called nucleons.
  • The atomic number (Z) is the number of protons. The mass number (A) is the total number of nucleons.
  • Isotopes have the same Z but different numbers of neutrons. Unstable isotopes are called radioisotopes.
  • The strong nuclear force binds nucleons together but has a very short range. It must overcome the electromagnetic repulsion between protons.
  • Binding energy is the energy equivalent of the mass defect (E = mc²). Higher binding energy per nucleon means greater stability.
  • Stable nuclei form a "valley of stability" on an N-Z plot. Nuclei above the valley are neutron-rich; nuclei below are proton-rich.
  • Alpha decay emits a helium-4 nucleus. Beta-minus decay emits an electron. Beta-plus decay emits a positron. Gamma decay emits a high-energy photon.
  • Half-life (t₁/₂) is the time for half the nuclei in a sample to decay. The decay follows N(t) = N₀(½)^(t/t₁/₂).
  • Activity is measured in becquerels (Bq): 1 Bq = 1 decay per second.
  • Radioisotopes have applications in medicine (imaging, therapy), archaeology (carbon dating), energy (nuclear power), and safety (smoke detectors).
